# Muse Wonder Full (max): Ambition reflections

## Reflections on the article

Beneath the four types, the essay makes one claim: the bottleneck has moved from execution to framing. When you hand a model a task, you are usually handing it your best guess at an intermediate step — make these slides, fix this crash, summarise this report — inherited from a world where execution was expensive, expertise was siloed, and somebody else had already digested the raw material for you. Speed keeps you on that middle layer and just does the guessed step faster. The other three types are escapes from the middle in three directions: Altitude goes up to the outcome ("what do you want to happen next?"), Depth goes down to the substrate (raw data, real instruments, first principles), and Range goes sideways into methods from fields you would never have hired. Read that way, "be more ambitious" is not motivational advice. It is an instruction to distrust the layer at which you habitually phrase requests, because that layer encodes old prices: what used to cost a team, a quarter, or a specialist.

The self-efficacy section explains why we stay on the middle layer. Model capability is jagged — "astounding in some directions, while inadequate in others" — and it moves every few weeks, so your prior about what is possible is almost always stale and almost always too pessimistic in the astounding directions. The Dantzig and Bannister stories are really about difficulty priors: people calibrate effort and even attempt-selection to what they believe the problem is. The essay's sharpest implicit point is that models have difficulty priors too. Told a goal is unreasonable, a model satisfices, hedges, reaches for a markdown table; told a stretch target is normal and to keep going, it searches longer and reaches for real tools. Ambition, in practice, is a two-person belief problem: the human must lend the model a higher prior, and then borrow confidence back from what the model demonstrably pulls off.

What is truest in the piece is the unglamorous plumbing. "Keep our apps crash-free" only became a sane request after crash reports were fetchable, versions mapped to commits, and machines existed on which fixes could be reproduced and verified. Likewise the warnings that carry the whole argument: insist on the methods and instruments of top practitioners rather than their vocabulary, never let the model judge itself, translate nouns into verbs before importing other fields, and distrust role-play that makes the transcript weirder without changing the work. Those four moves convert a slogan into something checkable. Without them, ambition is just a bigger prompt.

What the essay leaves out is cost. Generation has become cheap; verification, maintenance, attention, and accountability have not. An Altitude move that creates a durable human-out-of-the-loop workflow also creates a durable liability that needs credentials, monitoring, and an owner when the world changes. A Depth or Range move produces output in exactly the areas where the requester is least able to judge it — that is the point of borrowing expertise, and also the danger. And an outcome is often not yours to delegate: the quarterly deck may exist because a board, a regulator, or a customer wants that artifact, in that ritual, whatever a better system would look like. Altitude without authority is a career error dressed as vision. The essay notes that Altitude suits creative work poorly, but the limitation is broader. Where the outcome is contested, taste-laden, or political, delegating the outcome delegates values you have not specified, and the model will fill the gap with plausible median values.

There is also an unresolved tension between the two self-efficacy hacks. Locke and Latham say specific hard goals beat "do your best"; *The Inner Game of Tennis* says trying very hard is how you play badly, and Linux began as permission to fail. Both can be true if sequenced: a hard, specific target to mobilise search, held inside cheap, disposable attempts that neither party has to defend. Without that sequencing, hard goals have known failure modes the essay does not mention — tunnel vision, gaming the metric, and, for models in particular, confabulated success. A model pressed past the evidence does not usually stop; it reports victory. That is why the outside judge is not one tip among many but the load-bearing wall: belief hacks are only safe when something other than the believer can say no.

Finally, the essay underrates Speed and overrates how self-evident the astounding directions are. Speed dismissed as mere economics is often what makes Depth affordable: when a probe costs minutes instead of days, you can test ten assumptions against raw data instead of arguing about one. And nobody is handed a map of the jagged frontier. Finding it requires a habit the essay gestures at with Bannister — watching what others have pulled off, copying one feat, keeping your own list — plus cheap probes of your own. Ambition without probing is just hoping your bold direction happens to be one where the model is astounding rather than inadequate. The mature version of the advice is not "ask for more." It is: reframe up, ground down, borrow sideways, judge from outside, and buy the information about whether it works with the smallest probe that could prove you wrong.

## Five questions

### Q1: How do you tell real ambition from scope inflation?

By what increases. Inflation increases nouns: more features, more pages, more agents, a grander rewrite. Ambition increases one of three things — outcome ownership, evidence quality, or reuse — while making failure *more* visible, not less. "Build a churn dashboard with alerts and a new data pipeline" is inflation. "Find the earliest signal that predicts churn on data you have not seen, starting from the raw logs" is ambition, even though the deliverable might be smaller. The same test exposes fake versions of each type. Fake Altitude is an outcome so vague ("grow the business") that no decision or loop follows from it. Fake Depth is expert vocabulary and fancier tooling without rawer material or a harder check. Fake Range is a cast of personas chatting instead of imported methods doing work.

Four operational checks help before you commit. First, can the agent demonstrably fail without you taste-judging the result — is there a test, holdout, reproduction, or backtest that could return a clear no? If the only judge is your nod, you have not raised ambition, you have delegated judgement. Second, does the ambitious version produce the original deliverable as a byproduct or fallback, so you are never worse off than the modest request? Third, if it succeeds, does the *next* similar task get cheaper — because you now own a dataset, a harness, a simulation, a loop — or did you merely get one bigger artifact? Fourth, would a top practitioner recognise the method, or only the tone? A request that passes those checks can be as small as you like and still be ambitious. "Try to reproduce our belief about churn from raw data, adversarially, this afternoon" is tiny in scope and deep in exactly the sense that matters. Depth is scale-free; inflation is not.

### Q2: How do Altitude, Depth, and Range interact — and in what order should you apply them?

They pull in different directions, which is why applying all three at once produces a bloated, incoherent mega-task. Altitude abstracts away from the task ("never mind the deck, catch problems early"); Depth concretises into the substrate ("never mind the summary, give me the raw logs and a statistical model"). The resolution is a barbell: abstract about ends, concrete about evidence, and suspicious of everything in the middle — prescribed steps and pre-digested inputs. Range then spends from a different budget entirely: every imported field multiplies the methods you must sanity-check and the judges you must build, and it dilutes Depth if it arrives before you know what you are deep *about*.

So the default order is Altitude, then Depth, then Range, rationed. Altitude first, because Depth applied to the wrong outcome just optimises the wrong thing brilliantly. Depth second, because it reveals which blind spots are real; Range last, importing only the one to three methods that address a named blind spot, each paying its way with a concrete artifact or check. Two qualifications matter in practice. If the outcome is fixed by mandate — the board really does require that deck — skip Altitude-as-replacement and use Altitude only to clarify which decisions the artifact must force, then go deep. And do not automate (the durable form of Altitude) until Depth has validated the method, or you will freeze a shallow metric into a workflow that pursues it forever. In a multi-agent setup the division of labour follows the same logic: parallelise Range probes and adversarial Depth checks, which are genuinely independent, but keep the Altitude choice itself with one coherent owner — usually you — because a committee of sub-agents produces five outcomes and no decision.

### Q3: Whose self-efficacy is the bottleneck — the human's or the model's?

Both, coupled in a loop. The human's prior sets the size of the request; the model's prior sets how much search, tool use, and persistence the request receives. Models are trained on a world where most asks are modest, and they match effort accordingly: they satisfice, declare things impractical, produce the digested-table version. None of this is belief in the human sense — it is conditioning — but it responds to the same levers. A specific numeric stretch target, an existence proof ("here is a repo / paper / prior run where this worked"), removal of fake impossibility (install the tool, grant the access, fetch the raw data instead of the export), and an explicit "keep going, this is solvable" all shift the distribution toward longer, more serious attempts. The human then borrows efficacy back vicariously, Bannister-style, from the model's demonstrated feat and from other people's. Each side's evidence updates the other's prior.

This is also where the advice turns dangerous, because pressing a model past the evidence rarely produces refusal; it produces fluent claims of success. So a model "can't" needs diagnosis before it needs exhortation. There are at least four kinds: genuinely beyond current capability in that jagged direction; missing tool, data, or access; missing judge, so the model cannot tell a good attempt from a bad one; and underspecified outcome, where "can't" means "won't guess your values." Only the effort-prior kind responds to pep talks, and even then the pep talk is legitimate only when paired with an outside judge that can contradict it. The practical synthesis: lend belief freely at the level of attempts — label probes disposable, give permission to fail cheaply, set the unreasonable target — and lend none at the level of conclusions, where only the judge speaks. Ambition is a property of the search, not of the self-assessment.

### Q4: What does ambition cost, and when is staying small the right call?

The bill arrives in currencies the essay barely mentions. Verification is the big one: the deeper and wider you ask, the more the output lives in territory where you borrowed expertise precisely because you lack it, so checking gets harder as generation gets cheaper — judgement becomes the scarce resource. Maintenance is second: a crash-free loop, a churn simulation, a replacement review system all need plumbing, credentials, and an owner when inputs drift. Attention is third: parallel agents multiply output faster than they multiply your ability to review it, and unreviewed ambitious output is inventory, not leverage. Then risk and politics: a bigger blast radius when wrong, and outcome-level delegation that exceeds your actual authority. Finally, deskilling: if you delegate the outcome before you can judge the outcome, you may never acquire the judgement, which matters because supervision is the job ambition leaves you.

A workable rule follows: be ambitious only as far as you can judge, maintain, and own the consequences — and buy your way further with probes rather than commitments. Stay deliberately small when the real goal is learning, when no judge exists or can be built, when failure is costly or irreversible, when the task is a mandated artifact or a relationship (the apology, the negotiation) where the making is the meaning, or when the task is trivial and Speed is honestly enough. But small need not mean timid. The pattern that respects both ambition and cost is: ambitious direction, disposable first step, run beside the current way rather than instead of it, expand only after the probe passes its judge. The essay's own best example does exactly this — run the new review beside the quarterly deck for a quarter and let history arbitrate. Generalised, that is the safety pattern for all four types.

### Q5: What does ambition look like when there is no outside judge — in writing, research, design, or strategy?

This is where the essay's strongest rule is hardest to obey, because code has tests and churn data has holdouts, but an essay, a research synthesis, or a strategy has no ground truth waiting offstage. The answer is not to abandon the rule but to construct partial judges and be honest about the remainder. Backtests work more often than expected: require the strategy, rubric, or predictive signal to be stated in advance and scored against history it did not use. Predictions can be sealed: "state what this analysis implies we will observe by a date, before looking." Sources can be triangulated: every load-bearing claim resolved to a primary source that actually says it, checked by an independent thread given the sources but not the draft's conclusions. Baselines can be blind-compared: ambitious draft versus the modest one, judged on criteria written before either existed, on a sample small enough for real human judgement. An adversarial other-model pass is a weak judge, but a genuinely independent one — different starting material, different instructions, rewarded for finding the flaw — is far stronger than self-critique in the same thread.

When no judge can be constructed, ambition should change type rather than inflate. Do not aim for a single confident deliverable; aim for a map — the options, the cruxes that would decide between them, the strongest countercase, and what evidence would settle it. Range earns its keep here as cross-examination, not decoration: the point of the short seller is a method (follow incentives, check base rates, look for what must be hidden), and the point of the journalist is a method too (who says this, how would they know, what is the document). Require the agent to label what is verified, what is inferred, and what is taste. Fluency is not evidence, length is not depth, and in judge-less domains the most ambitious honest output is often the one that makes its own uncertainty usable — small early samples to calibrate your taste cheaply beat one enormous polished artifact you can only accept or reject whole.

## The prompt

```
Before building anything, help me be more ambitious about this task — deeper and higher-leverage, not just bigger.

Look first at what's actually here (files, data, tools). Assume you can use the shell, install software, and run experiments. Check before assuming something isn't possible.

Then briefly work through (a few lines each):
1. Altitude — What outcome do I want next: what decision, change, or repeatable loop should this produce? State the task as verbs (what it must do, for whom), and say what would make the task as written unnecessary or automatic — plus any data access or plumbing it would need.
2. Depth — What would a top practitioner do that I'm not asking for? Name the rawest material they'd start from, the real methods and tools they'd use, one of my assumptions to test against that material, and one blind spot it might reveal. Do what experts do; don't role-play them. If quality is measurable, set one specific stretch target and treat it as solvable until evidence says otherwise.
3. Range — Which 1–3 other fields bring a useful method (not a persona) here? Name each method and what it would catch or create that the obvious approach misses.
4. Judge and brake — What outside check (test, reproduction, holdout, backtest, simulation, independent cross-check) would show the result is right, other than you or me saying so? If none exists, say so and give the closest proxy. Name the main way the ambitious version could be worse, and the smallest cheap probe to run first.

Reply with: (a) my task in one line; (b) one copy-pastable rewritten prompt asking for exactly the ambitious version — including where parallel sub-agents would run genuinely independent work, such as an adversarial check; (c) at most 5 bullets on what changed and the trade-off. Prefer deeper evidence over more scope. Do not execute yet; wait for my go-ahead.

My task (and any fixed constraints): [describe it here]
```

## Design notes

The prompt keeps the essay's three substantive moves but turns each into a question the agent must answer from the actual workspace, in the order Altitude, Depth, Range, so the outcome is chosen before methods are borrowed and Range stays rationed to one to three *methods*, never personas. Depth is explicitly defined against bigness — "deeper evidence over more scope," rawest material, one assumption tested, one blind spot sought — which is the essay's own Depth test compressed into a single step. The self-efficacy hack is included but tethered: check before assuming impossibility, set one specific stretch target, treat it as solvable *until evidence says otherwise*. Belief is lent to the search, not to the conclusion.

Two additions go beyond the essay because daily agent work needs them. The outside judge is mandatory, with an honesty clause ("if none exists, say so") so writing and strategy tasks get a proxy and a labelled remainder instead of fake validation. And every reframing must name its brake — the main way the ambitious version could be worse, plus the smallest cheap probe to run first. That probe is where permission to fail lives, and it keeps ambition from becoming an irreversible commitment or an unreviewable pile of parallel output. Parallel sub-agents are mentioned only inside the rewritten prompt and only for genuinely independent work such as an adversarial check, to avoid multi-agent theatre during the reframing itself.

Deliberately left out: Speed (the default mode needs no prompt), inspirational framing and crew fantasies (they invite role-play), domain checklists (they would make the prompt task-specific and long), and any instruction to execute. The output is capped — a few lines per step, at most five trade-off bullets, one rewrite, then a hard stop for the user's go-ahead — because a prompt that returns an essay every time will stop being pasted. Fixed constraints are folded into the task slot rather than given their own form, keeping the whole thing to one paste and one decision.
